The part nobody quotes

A sign is the last
step in a chain
of other people's
decisions.

You are buying a piece of aluminium, acrylic or vinyl. What you are actually paying for is everything that has to line up before it gets made: the address, the artwork version, the landlord's criteria, the city, the material call, the access to the wall, the power at the wall.

Each of those steps is reasonable on its own. The expensive confusion usually turns up between them — a measurement, a version, an assumption or an owner quietly changes as the job moves from one party to the next. It stays quiet until it shows up in metal, vinyl, light or labour.

Not every sign problem is a handoff problem. Workmanship and material choice cause their share. But the handoff is the one nobody puts on the quote.

How a job runs here

Seven checkpoints,
in this order.

Nothing exotic. It is the order that keeps a sign from being made twice — and it is the order the sign beside you is being built in.

Layout

  1. 01

    Brief

    What you need, where it goes, when it has to be up, and why that date matters. Five minutes of questions here removes most of the guessing later.

  2. 02

    Site facts

    Address, mounting surface, access, power, operating hours. If the job needs a survey before anyone can quote it honestly, we say so instead of guessing a number.

  3. 03

    Files and specification

    We open the artwork before we price it. A raster logo blown up to twelve inches, a missing bleed, a colour build that will not hold on vinyl — you get a call. An online printer would print it and invoice you.

  4. 04

    Approval map

    Who has to sign off: you, your corporate brand team, the landlord, the municipality. We lay out the path and prepare what we can. We do not promise how a city will rule.

  5. 05

    Production release

    One approved version goes into production, and that version is the one that gets made. Approvals in a group chat are where the wrong file gets built.

  6. 06

    Delivery or installation

    Pickup, delivery, or installation depending on the job and the site. Install dates get confirmed against access, power and your operating hours — not just against our schedule.

  7. 07

    Acceptance and record

    A walkthrough or photos, a punch list if something needs correcting, and the specs kept on file. The next location, the next van or the reorder starts from that record instead of from scratch.

Some of these steps we run ourselves and some we coordinate with trade partners. Which is which gets told to you before you commit to anything — see who owns what.

Before you ask

The questions
we get most.

If yours is not here, call. It is a short conversation.

How much is it?

Most work is priced on size, material, quantity and installation scope, so a real number needs the dimensions and the site. Send those in the brief and you get a quote rather than a range. We are competitive, but we do not compete on being the cheapest shop in the county — cheap material on a storefront tends to get replaced twice.

How fast can you turn it around?

Simple print work can sometimes be ready the next day or the day after. Fabricated, illuminated, permitted or fleet work takes longer, and the timeline usually depends on how quickly artwork and approvals come back rather than on production.

Tell us the deadline and why it matters. If it cannot be met honestly, we will say so before you commit — and if you came to us late, we will still try to work with it rather than tell you it is your problem.

Do you actually install, or just print?

We deliver, we do pickup, and we install. Installation scope depends on height, access, mounting surface and electrical requirements; some installs are run with trade partners. That gets confirmed on your project rather than assumed.

Is everything made in house?

No, and we would rather say so. Printing and graphics production are ours. Some fabrication and electrical work is done with trade partners. You will know which parts of your job sit with a partner before you sign anything.

What if my artwork is wrong?

You get a phone call. Vector or press-ready files are ideal — PDF or the original vector artwork. If all you have is a logo pulled off a website, tell us; often it can be rebuilt, and it is far cheaper to fix a file than to reprint a wrap.

Can you keep twelve locations looking the same?

That is the point of keeping a record. Approved specification, material, colour build and dimensions stay on file so location nine matches location one, and a reorder does not restart the whole conversation.

Do you handle permits?

We prepare and coordinate what we reasonably can, and we will tell you what a given jurisdiction is likely to require. We do not guarantee a permit outcome and we do not give legal advice — the municipality decides, and Miami-Dade, Broward and Palm Beach cities do not all decide the same way.

We already have a sign vendor.

Then keep them for what is working. A sensible way to test another shop is one location, one vehicle or one print run — a job with a real deadline but a contained downside. If it goes well, the next one is easy. If it does not, you have lost one job, not a rollout.

You are a newer company. Why take the risk?

Fair question, and the honest answer is that the entity is new and the trade experience behind it is not. Start with something scoped small, ask us for work relevant to your job, and judge the shop on how the first project is communicated rather than on how long the LLC has existed.
